# Decision log

| ID | Decision | Reason | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | Build a stationary gargoyle, not a moving statue | A wheeled base adds drivetrain, balance and collision risk around children without adding to the machine-learning content | Accepted |
| D2 | Use both UNO Q processors with clear roles | The MPU runs models and logic; the MCU owns real-time output and the safe state | Accepted |
| D3 | Edge Impulse models make the decisions; the language model only writes | Keeps behaviour measurable and lets every model be compared with a simple baseline | Accepted |
| D4 | Generate dialogue ahead of time and pre-render speech | Measured generation speed on the board is too slow to run during a visit | Accepted |
| D5 | Gemma 3 1B for the line bank, capped at 3 threads | It is the open-weight model available to the App Lab runner. 3 threads leaves a core for the other models; a 16 minute run at 4 threads did not reset the board, so the earlier reset claim is unverified (docs/benchmarks/llm-threads.md) | Accepted |
| D6 | Gate the sound and keyword models behind a visitor detector | Saves CPU and prevents false starts when nobody is present | Accepted |
| D7 | Candy path uses a tilted sweet tube, one servo pocket paddle (load and dump angles) and an IR break-beam sensor. The mouth is fixed open and the tongue is the slide, so there is no jaw. Replaces the pocket wheel and stepper, and the jaw servo | One servo and no driver board, no step counting and no moving part near hands. Releases one candy at a time and confirms the drop without machine learning. Best with one type of wrapped sweet; if it jams in bench tests with the real sweet, the pocket wheel is the fallback | Accepted, tube angle (60 degrees or steeper) and paddle angles to be set on a printed prototype with 50 dispenses |
| D8 | No accelerometer model | The break-beam and a timeout already handle jams, and a threshold handles tampering | Accepted |
| D9 | Costume feature is core. It speaks only when confident and defaults to silence | Costume-aware roasts are a headline feature, and staying silent when unsure avoids wrong or unwelcome comments | Accepted |
| D10 | Riddles use a small fixed answer set | Keyword spotting is reliable on a few words and unreliable on open speech from children | Accepted |
| D11 | Training data from consenting adult volunteers only | Privacy; cloud tools receive no images of children | Accepted |
| D12 | Nothing is stored during play and nothing leaves the device | Privacy, and it works with no network | Accepted |
| D13 | Riddle keyword spotting is a core feature, not optional | Vision, keyword spotting, LEDs and a language model are the cascade the project is built around | Accepted |
| D14 | One audience setting for everyone: content written for children, teenagers and young people, no age or size classification | Visitors are a mix and adults enjoy it too. Skipping the split avoids collecting child data and avoids misreading a short adult or a tall child (D11) | Accepted |
| D15 | Final build powers the board from a 45 W USB-C PD charger through a USB-C hub with PD pass-through; LEDs, servo and speakers stay on their own 5 V rail | The board has one USB-C port, so power and peripherals share the hub. Every benchmark so far used a 5 V 3 A iPhone charger and a Portronics Mport 51 whose pass-through rating is unknown (docs/benchmarks/cascade-load.md) | Proposed, check the hub rating and log the current with a USB meter |
| D16 | Costume training images come from web photos of adults (Openverse and Wikimedia Commons), with source, licence and author logged in a manifest; any accuracy claim for the deployed gargoyle needs a test set of real camera frames | No volunteers are available for photos and this is a hobby build, so all licences are accepted. Web photos are posed and well lit, so accuracy on them does not predict accuracy in front of the gargoyle camera. Children are excluded as in D11. The repo holds neither the images nor the fetch script; the manifest of sources and licences stays local | Accepted, amends D11 for the costume images only (sound data rules unchanged) |
| D17 | Costume list stays at the 11 classes; no more are added | More classes need more training images and lower per-class accuracy on a small camera dataset | Accepted |
| D18 | Spine is eight protruding translucent plates, tail to head like a dinosaur back, with three WS2812B pixels each (24 pixels on their own data pin, D32), filled tail to head as the scream gets louder, or counting down during a riddle (D28) | Plates give the dorsal-fin look. The meter fills whole plates, eight steps the visitor can read from a distance | Accepted, plate sizes and spacing to be set when the body is designed |
| D19 | Mouth fog from a 5 V ultrasonic mist module on a small water cup with a wick, in a sealed chamber. A small fan pushes the fog up a 10 mm OD silicone tube to a port in the roof of the mouth, angled 45 degrees forward and down so it rolls out over the tongue. The MCU switches it in short bursts, never during LISTEN, and the watchdog safe state turns it off | Mist adds the eerie look. It is cold, wet and noisy, so it stays away from the mic, the sweets and the boards, and a dead MPU must not leave it running | Accepted. Port printed in the face piece (8 mm exit, 10.4 mm socket). Current draw, burst length and how far the fog travels up the tube to be measured on the build |
| D20 | Eyes are two 7-pixel WS2812 round boards behind translucent PLA windows, 5 to 10 mm behind the skin, each in a dark collar; the two eye rings make one chain of 14 pixels on D5 and the spine has its own chain on D6 (D32) | Glowing eyes suit a stone figure, use far fewer pixels than 8x8 panels, and share the library with the spine. Window thickness and the brightness cap are set from a printed swatch and a meter reading | Accepted, replaces the two 8x8 panels; the windows are now printed in place (D24) |
| D21 | Camera sits high on the plinth front, to one side of the tongue, tilted up 10 to 15 degrees behind a carved opening, with the display table raised so the lens is about 1.0 m off the floor | Keeps the lens out of the mouth mist, shortens the USB run and leaves the head to the eye boards. The view is about 30 cm lower than a head camera, so it looks up at adults, and the costume images (D16) were taken at eye level | Superseded by D23 |
| D22 | The gargoyle sits on the plinth on a flat joint and is held by four M3 screws driven up from inside the plinth into heat-set inserts in the gargoyle's base. Each screw is reached with a ball-end key through a bore from the bottom door. Board power and data come in through a USB-C panel-mount socket in a 14 x 7.5 mm hole low on the back | The joint has to open again for service, so it gets inserts rather than glue. Nothing shows from outside, and the statue keeps its weathered stone look | Accepted, insert pockets, screw paths and the USB hole checked on the model; fit to be checked on the first print |
| D23 | Camera sits in the forehead as a third eye: an Arducam B0490 USB camera in its metal case, held only by its 13.9 mm lens in a 14.2 mm bore through the centre of the sculpted forehead spiral, aimed straight ahead | The lens looks out from the eye line of the figure, away from the mouth mist, and the spiral hides it. No bracket: the case rests on a thin nose inside the sleeve | Accepted, replaces D21; sizes from vendor pages and calipers, bore fit to be checked on the first print |
| D24 | Each eye is a thin transparent PLA+ cap printed in place with the head in one multi-material print. Only the visible front of the cap is transparent; the part tucked under the lids is grey and part of the head. The LED board sits in an open pocket behind it | One print, no glued inserts, and the eye looks the same from outside. Less transparent filament and fewer colour changes: the transparent volume per eye dropped from 3.03 to 0.69 cm3 (measured on the mesh, not yet printed) | Accepted, look to be checked on the first print |
| D25 | The dispenser drops onto a locating pin and three posts inside the head and is held by three M3 x 10 thread-forming screws in 2.6 mm pilot holes. It goes in before the head top is closed. Sweets are refilled through a lid in the head dome cut along a hairline crack, held by four pairs of 6 x 2 mm disc magnets | A soldering iron cannot reach the posts square on, so heat-set inserts are out. Screws into plain pilots need no tool beyond a driver. The lid takes up to 5 sweets in the tube with nothing visible outside | Accepted, pilot size (2.5 to 2.8 mm) and magnet hold to be checked on a printed test piece |
| D26 | Two speakers, one against each end wall of the plinth on its own glued tray, face each other on the same line, front of centre. Each plays through a patch of small worn holes (0.75 to 1.3 mm, about 375 per wall, frayed edge, 130 to 150 mm across) over its own face only. The rest of each wall is the original sculpt. The -x speaker covers the key line to its front joint screw, so it is lifted out through the door before that screw is undone. The base is cut flat with no seam ring. Each ear bowl has 9 small holes into the head for the mic and the sound sensor | Left and right channels match the viewer's left and right. The holes read as weathering, so nothing mechanical shows, and the flat base prints without supports | Accepted, loudness through the holes to be checked on the build |
| D27 | The statue and the plinth print in pieces that fit the P2S plate (256 mm). Each piece is cut by one axis plane where it cuts the least stone, never through the eyes, the lid, the spine plates, the ear and camera holes, the dispenser or the screw bosses. Every seam is glued and lined up with at least two 1.75 mm filament pins in 2 mm holes, 6 mm deep on each side. A bit under 3 cm3 that a cut would leave loose goes back onto the piece next to it. The head cap with both eyes is one two-colour print, the spine plates print in transparent PLA+, and the wings print last on their own plates | No piece of the statue at 1.18x fits the plate whole. Short cuts mean short glue lines and less sculpt detail lost, pins stop the halves sliding while the glue sets, and the face and body can be assembled before the wings arrive | Accepted, pin fit and seam lines to be checked on the first prints |
| D28 | The challenge is a scream or a riddle. A scream is judged on loudness alone: arecord on the camera mic, 0.2 s RMS mapped from dBFS to 0 to 100, shown live on the spine, and won when it lights the 8th (top) plate (level 94) on 2 readings in a row. A scream that never gets there is too quiet. The riddle keeps the keyword spotter and the spine counts down. The sound classifier and its brick are removed | The sound classifier scored 45.6 percent in Studio, and a visitor can see a meter and push it, where a classifier verdict is a guess. Two readings in a row stop one bang from winning | Accepted, dB range and the win level to be set on the board at the fixed mic gain |
| D29 | The props head crop finds faces with YuNet on onnxruntime (`app/python/yunet.py`, onnxruntime in `app/python/requirements.txt`) instead of `cv2.FaceDetectorYN`. The deployed `yunet.onnx` has a symbolic input size so it runs at the frame's own 640 x 480 shape, as OpenCV does. The costume and props calls run on their own thread, and a new visitor is reported once their first label is in, or after 2 s | The App Lab OpenCV (4.13.0, Arduino build) has no dnn module, so `cv2.FaceDetectorYN.create` fails at start. Replacing that OpenCV would shadow the build the bricks use. On 131 PC images resized to 640 x 480, all 257 faces of 20 px and up came back within 1 percent of a face height of OpenCV's boxes. On the board, with the app running, YuNet took 492 ms per frame at a fixed 640 x 640 and 200 ms at 640 x 480. A full props call took 825 ms (median of 10 on one test frame). Smaller detector sizes miss faces and move the boxes, and training cropped at 640. Run inside the brick callback, the label lands after the visit wakes (0.3 s), so the greeting would mostly fall back to the generic tease (worked out from the timings, not seen in a visit) | Accepted |
| D30 | The keyword spotter keeps its expert-mode Keras script (MFCC 13, conv1d 32-64-64 with BN, time and coefficient masks plus a small time shift, label smoothing 0.1, per-class loss weights set by hand) instead of Studio's visual blocks | The same network built from Studio blocks, with Studio's spectrogram augmentation, scored 86.12% (auto class weights) and 86.82% (no class weights) on the 569 held-out clips, against 90.51% for the script. That gap is about 4 points; the run-to-run spread is about 1.1. Auto weights let `unknown` win too little and no weights let it win too much; the script's middle weighting and label smoothing have no visual setting, and Studio's augmentation fit only 32 cycles in the 1 h compute limit against 90 for the script. Studio float32 test, 2026-11-24 | Superseded by D31 |
| D31 | The keyword spotter is built from Studio blocks only: MFE (20 ms frames, 10 ms stride, 40 filters, FFT 256) into Studio's Transfer Learning (Keyword Spotting) block, MobileNetV2 0.35 with a 128 neuron final layer and 0.1 dropout, auto class weights, 90 cycles, learning rate 0.001, no augmentation. It replaces the expert script of D30 | It scored 93.15% (530/569) on the 569 held-out clips against 90.51% for the script, and 94.52% against 84.25% on the 146 clean old clips. A repeat training gave the same 530/569 (Studio training is seeded). The x86_64 .eim agreed with Studio on all 569 clips. Studio's spectrogram augmentation crashes with this block, and the block only accepts the default MFE settings. In Docker on a PC the network was about 4 times slower than the script's (median 51 to 59 ms against 13 to 14 ms per call, 100 clips, two passes); the script took 29.6 ms per window on the board, so about 100 ms is expected there against the 50 ms target. Studio float32 test, 2026-11-24 | Accepted, latency and accuracy of the deployed .eim to be measured on the board; the previous model is kept for rollback |
| D32 | No level shifter. D5 drives the two 7-pixel eye rings (14 pixels) and D6 drives the spine: a WS2812B strip cut at the pads into 24 single pixels, wired as eight plates of three, tail first. Each data line has a 330 to 470 ohm resistor at its first pixel, and a 1000 uF capacitor sits across 5 V and GND where power enters the chains | The finished build runs both chains from the 3.3 V MCU pins with no 74AHCT125 and no glitches seen. The WS2812B datasheet asks for 0.7 x VDD (3.5 V at 5 V) on DIN, so 3.3 V is below the spec. It works on this build with short first data wires; a long first wire or a different strip batch could need the shifter back. Two pins keep each chain short and let the sketch redraw the eyes and spine separately | Accepted. Schematic rev B in hardware/kicad shows it. Replaces the single 30-pixel chain and the shifter from D18 and D20 |
